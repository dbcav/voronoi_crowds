Crowds on a Voronoi mesh

What’s a mean field game? It’s a way to model what happens when a huge number of "agents" each make their own best decisions in a shared space (or "play a game"). Instead of tracking every individual, you track the crowd as a density and look for a consistent outcome: each agent’s plan is optimal given where the crowd will be, and the crowd ends up exactly where those plans take it.

More on mean field games

The description above intentionally leaves many things up to interpretation. "Agents" might be pedestrians, quadrotor drones, self-driving cars, or traders in a financial market, and "games" might be a crowd evacuation problem, coordinated exploration of a 3-d region, or a group of traders each trying to liquidate a large position without causing a crash in the price.

By alternating between solving for the crowd's density and the cost it creates, we can iteratively improve until we reach a fixed point. That fixed point is often called an "equilibrium", and I spend time trying to find new ways to compute it reliably and efficiently.

Here we'll attempt to model how a collection of pedestrians would move through a room in 2-D. There are also three different obstacle configurations to play with, and different repulsion terms available.

What's a Voronoi cell?

Imagine scattering some points in a room. Then the Voronoi cell of a point is all of the floor that is closer to that point than to any other point. Every spot in the room belongs to exactly one cell (apart from ties on the boundaries), so the cells tile the room with no gaps and no overlaps. Each cell is a convex polygon, and two points whose cells share an edge are called neighbours.

What makes this useful for crowds is that a cell's area is a local measure of crowding. An agent packed into a tight group gets a small cell and an agent with room around it gets a large one, so one over the area behaves like a density that each agent can work out from its neighbours alone. In the simulation below each cell is also cut off at a small radius around its agent, so a lone agent doesn't claim the whole empty room.

The page uses Voronoi cells in two ways. In the first simulation the points are the agents themselves, so the cells reshape at every time step and the pressure pushes each agent toward roomier neighbours. In the second, the points stay fixed and their cells become the mesh on which the mean field game is solved, much like a finite volume grid that can follow walls and doorways more naturally than a square grid can.

What the page computes

A gentler walk through the game, what every slider changes and how it is solved is on How the crowd game works.

Sheet 1: one energy, local forces

Agent \(i\) owns \(W_i\), its Voronoi cell cut off at a bubble of radius \(R\). With a comfortable area \(a_0\), its crowding is \(\rho_i = a_0/|W_i|\) and its pressure is \(P_i = \rho_i^6\). The whole crowd has one energy

$$E = \sum_i \frac{|W_i|\,\rho_i^{6}}{5},$$

and its gradient only involves neighbours:

$$-\frac{\partial E}{\partial x_i} = \sum_j \frac{S_{ij}}{d_{ij}}\,(P_i - P_j)\,(c_{ij} - x_i) \;+\; P_i \oint_{\text{arc}} n \,\mathrm{d}s.$$

Here \(S_{ij}\) is the edge two cells share, \(c_{ij}\) its midpoint and \(d_{ij}\) the distance between the two agents. The last integral runs over the arc where a cell meets its bubble. Agents move along this force plus their walking direction, so the crowd slides toward roomier neighbours. Contact repulsion is the usual alternative: a push between any two agents closer than a few body widths.

Step 1: backward, then forward with the transpose

The mesh has cells \(i\) with areas \(|V_i|\) and edge weights \(\tau_{ij} = S_{ij}/d_{ij}\). The cost still to pay, \(u_i(t)\), solves backward in time

$$-\dot u_i + \frac{1}{2|V_i|}\sum_j \tau_{ij}\big((u_i - u_j)^+\big)^2 - \frac{\nu}{|V_i|}\sum_j \tau_{ij}\,(u_j - u_i) = \kappa\, m_i,$$

ending at the walking distance to the target. Agents leave cell \(i\) for \(j\) at speed \((u_i - u_j)^+/d_{ij}\). The density \(m\) then moves forward using exactly the transpose of the matrix from each backward step. That one choice makes mass exactly conserved and density never negative. It also makes the average cost at the start equal the total cost paid along the way, to solver precision. It is the same transpose trick that backpropagation uses.

Fictitious play couples the two: solve backward against the crowd guessed so far, push the crowd forward, average it in, repeat. Crowd-blind agents are the same game with \(\kappa = 0\).

Steps 2 and 3: from the density back to agents

A planning agent in cell \(i\) moves with the equilibrium’s drift

$$v_i = \frac{1}{|V_i|}\sum_j S_{ij}\,(u_i - u_j)^+\, n_{ij} \;\approx\; -\nabla u,$$

blended smoothly across neighbouring cells. On top of that it gets the spread of the equilibrium’s own random walk, whose covariance per unit time is \(\sum_j \lambda_{ij}\, d_{ij}^2\, n_{ij} n_{ij}^{\mathsf T}\) with jump rates \(\lambda_{ij} = \tau_{ij}\big((u_i-u_j)^+ + \nu\big)/|V_i|\). That spread includes \(\nu\) plus the smoothing the upwind scheme builds in, so \(N\) planning agents reproduce the discrete equilibrium rather than a sharper idealized version of it.

A reacting agent uses the same drift formula with the cost to go replaced by the terminal cost alone, which is a zero-horizon plan, and adds the Voronoi pressure from sheet 1. Every agent is billed

$$\int_0^T \Big(\tfrac12 |v|^2 + \kappa\, \hat m(t, X_t)\Big)\,\mathrm{d}t \;+\; \text{urgency} \times D(X_T),$$

where \(\hat m\) is the crowd’s own density on the mesh, pooled over each cell and its neighbours, with the agent itself left out.

Closing comments

You probably noticed that the planning crowd doesn't always beat the reacting crowd on the numbers in the table. That's expected, for a few reasons. A mean field equilibrium is a Nash equilibrium: each agent does the best it can for itself given what everyone else does, which is not the same as the crowd doing the best it can as a whole, so a simpler rule can sometimes end up with a lower average cost. The equilibrium is also computed for a continuous density on a mesh with a limited number of rounds of play, and then handed to a few hundred individual agents who follow the plan without ever looking at each other, while the reacting agents respond to where their neighbours actually are. Some of the metrics, like the number of pairs touching, aren't part of the game's cost at all.

None of this means mean field games aren't useful. They aren't meant to be a complete replacement for multi-agent simulation. Their strength is that the cost of solving one doesn't grow directly with the number of agents, and the equilibrium is known to be an approximate equilibrium for the finite crowd, with the approximation getting better as the crowd gets larger. When the number of agents is overwhelming, that makes a mean field game a good way to find a rough starting point, and the two can be used in tandem: the mean field solution supplies the overall plan, and a multi-agent model refines it with the details the density leaves out, such as individual bodies bumping into each other.
